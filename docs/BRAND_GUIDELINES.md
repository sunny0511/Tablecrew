# TableCrew Brand Guidelines

## Purpose

This document defines who TableCrew is as a brand — the personality, voice, and visual identity principles that must stay consistent everywhere the company shows up: the app itself, the App Store and Play Store listings, the website, social channels, investor materials, and every marketing asset described in `MARKETING.md` and `SEO.md`. Where `DESIGN_SYSTEM.md` defines pixel-level execution (hex codes, type scale, component specs), this document defines the judgment calls that sit above execution: what TableCrew sounds like, what it stands for, and what it must never look or feel like.

Every principle here answers back to the same core tension identified across `USER_PERSONAS.md` and `VALUES.md`: TableCrew must feel warm and inviting enough for Maya (27, anxious about showing up alone) while feeling substantive and non-juvenile enough for Devon (38) and Grace (58) — and, above all, it must never be mistaken for a dating app. That last constraint is not a minor style note; it is a load-bearing brand requirement, because Grace's stated anxiety ("is this secretly a dating app?") represents a trust barrier that, if triggered, causes users to delete the app before ever hosting or joining a Table.

---

## 1. The Name Story

**TableCrew** is built from two words, each carrying deliberate weight, and both should appear in brand storytelling (About pages, App Store descriptions, press materials, investor narrative) as the etymology behind the product, not just its label.

**Table** is arguably the oldest cross-cultural social technology humans have. Long before written language, before markets, before cities, humans gathered around a shared surface to eat together — a bounded, physical space that requires everyone present to slow down, sit, and be with each other for a fixed span of time. Almost every culture on earth has some version of this: a hearth, a low table, a long table, a floor mat. The table is not a metaphor for connection — it *is* the original infrastructure for connection, and it has never gone out of style, which is precisely why it works as a brand anchor across the global, multi-generational audience TableCrew serves. Unlike a "feed" or a "network," a table implies a boundary: a fixed, small number of seats, a beginning and an end, mutual presence. That boundary is exactly what our product enforces (headcounts, defined time and place) and exactly what differentiates a Table from an infinite-scroll social feed.

**Crew** signals a small, trusted, ongoing group — sailors on a ship, a film production, a kitchen team. A crew is not a following or an audience; it is a bounded group of people who show up for each other repeatedly and know each other's names. That is precisely what our persistent Crew feature is: not a follower list, not a friend count, but a small recurring group you build a social life around.

Put together, **TableCrew** names the exact mechanic of the product: a small trusted group (Crew), gathering at a bounded, physical, time-limited surface (Table). The name should always be told as this two-part story — not as an abstract "connection" brand, but as a brand built on two very old, very concrete human ideas. This etymology is a useful antidote to the dating-app concern: dating apps are named after gestures (swipe, spark, match, tinder-as-kindling-for-romance); TableCrew is named after furniture and a group of people who work together. That naming register alone signals platonic, structured, no-agenda intent before a user reads a single line of copy.

## 2. Brand Personality and Archetype

**Recommended archetype: The Host (a variant of the Caregiver archetype, adapted for hospitality specifically).**

Brand archetype frameworks commonly used in consumer app branding include The Jester (playful, irreverent — Duolingo, many dating apps' tone), The Outlaw/Rebel (disruptive, rule-breaking — many nightlife and dating apps lean here to signal edge and spontaneity), The Explorer (discovery-driven, adventurous), and The Caregiver (nurturing, reassuring, other-focused). We recommend a specific, hospitality-flavored expression of the Caregiver: **The Host** — the archetype of someone who has set the table before you arrived, who introduces you to others with warmth, who notices if you're standing alone at the edge of the room and brings you in.

**Why not The Jester.** The Jester archetype (irreverent humor, memes, constant lightness) is the default register of most social and dating apps because it lowers social stakes through humor. But over-indexing on jokes and internet-native humor is precisely what makes a brand read as youth-coded and can feel dismissive to Grace or Devon, who are looking for something that takes their social life seriously, not something that treats connection as a punchline. A little wit is welcome (see Voice, section 3) but it is not the organizing personality.

**Why not The Outlaw/Rebel.** Many dating and nightlife-adjacent apps lean into "break the rules of dating," "disrupt the old way of meeting people" framing — provocative, edgy, a little dangerous. That energy is antithetical to Maya's need for safety signals and directly reinforces the "is this a dating app" fear, since edginess and romantic/sexual framing travel together in the category's existing visual and verbal language.

**Why The Host works.** A host's entire job is to make a guest feel expected, safe, and cared for — before, during, and after the gathering. This maps directly onto our product mechanics (a host sets time/place/headcount; the app's job is to support that host in making every guest feel welcome) and it maps onto the tone we want the *brand itself* to take toward the user: TableCrew, as a company, acts as a host to its users, not a platform extracting engagement from them. This is also a distinctive archetype in the category — almost no direct competitor (Meetup, Partiful, Bumble BFF, Timeleft) is built around hosting-as-brand-personality in this explicit a way, giving TableCrew clear differentiation.

Archetype in one sentence for internal use: **"We are the friend who already reserved the table and is waving you over."**

## 3. Voice Attributes

TableCrew's voice is: **warm, clear, encouraging, a little playful, and grounded.** Five adjectives, each defined against its nearest wrong turn:

- **Warm, not saccharine.** We write like someone glad to see you, not like a greeting card. Warmth shows up as specific, human phrasing ("Maya just RSVP'd — she's in") rather than generic cheer-language ("Yay! Someone's coming! 🎉🎉🎉").
- **Clear, not clinical.** We write in plain, short sentences a first-time user understands immediately. We are not a legal or enterprise-software product, so we never default to formal, distancing language ("Your reservation request has been submitted for processing") when a direct sentence will do ("You've asked to join — the host will confirm soon").
- **Encouraging, not hype-y.** We cheer users on toward showing up and hosting, but we never manufacture excitement with exclamation-point stacking, all-caps, or countdown-timer urgency ("HURRY! Only 2 spots left!!"). Encouragement here means removing friction and naming small wins honestly, not inflating stakes.
- **A little playful, not jokey.** Occasional light wit is welcome in low-stakes copy (an empty state, a loading message) but humor never appears in moments of vulnerability (a cancelled Table, a declined invite, a privacy setting) where a user needs to feel taken seriously.
- **Grounded, not corporate.** We sound like a thoughtful person who built this because they cared about the problem, not like a brand-safety committee. We avoid startup jargon ("leverage," "synergy," "unlock your social graph") and business-speak entirely.

### We Are This, Not That

In practice, here is how those five attributes resolve real copy decisions. When welcoming a new host to their first Table, **we are** direct and reassuring ("Your Table is live. Invite a few people, or leave it open for others to discover.") **not** breathless and hyperbolic ("Congrats!!! You're officially a TableCrew Host!!! 🎉🔥"). When a Table fills up, **we are** matter-of-fact and kind ("This Table is full — we'll let you know if a seat opens up.") **not** artificially urgent ("HURRY, spots are almost gone!"). When someone hasn't hosted in a while, **we are** a gentle nudge that respects their time ("It's been a few weeks since your last Table with the Downtown Crew. Want to set one up?") **not** a guilt trip ("Your friends miss you! Don't let them down 😢"). When explaining a privacy setting, **we are** plain and specific ("Only people you invite can see this Table.") **not** vague or legalistic ("Your visibility preferences will be applied in accordance with platform policy."). And when something goes wrong, **we are** warm and solution-oriented ("That didn't go through — mind trying again?") **not** cold and technical ("Error 400: Bad Request.").

The throughline across every one of these pairs: the "this" column treats the user as a guest whose time and feelings matter; the "not that" column treats the user as a metric to be moved or a ticket to be closed.

## 4. Logo Usage Principles

*(No image assets exist yet; the principles below govern how the logo must be designed and used once produced.)*

- **Wordmark-first.** The primary logo lockup is a wordmark set in a custom-adjusted version of the Fraunces display serif (see `DESIGN_SYSTEM.md`), not an abstract icon-first mark. A wordmark keeps the brand legible and readable at first encounter (App Store icon aside), which matters because an abstract icon can be misread or feel evasive about what the product actually is — clarity itself is a hospitality signal.
- **Icon mark for small spaces.** A secondary, simplified icon mark — a stylized overhead view of a small round table with place settings, rendered as simple line geometry — is used only where a full wordmark won't fit legibly (app icon, favicon, social avatar). The icon must never be used interchangeably with the wordmark in contexts where both would fit; the wordmark is always preferred when space allows.
- **Clear space.** Minimum clear space around the logo (wordmark or icon) equals the height of the capital "T" in the wordmark, measured on all four sides. No text, imagery, or competing graphic element may intrude into that space.
- **Minimum size.** The wordmark must never be reproduced smaller than 90px wide in digital contexts (or 0.75in in print), below which the Fraunces serif's fine details begin to blur or fill in. The icon mark alone has a smaller minimum (24px digital / 0.25in print) suitable for favicons and app-icon contexts.
- **Color usage.** The logo is produced in exactly three approved variants: full Ink Charcoal (`#2B241F`) on Linen Cream or white backgrounds, full Linen Cream (`#FBF6EF`) on Ink Charcoal or photographic dark backgrounds, and full Terracotta (`#C1653A`) for single-color accent placement (e.g., embossed on merchandise, watermarks). The logo is never reproduced in any other color, including brand-accent colors like Sage or Gold Ochre, and never as a gradient.
- **Misuse examples (must be avoided):** stretching or squashing the wordmark to fit a layout; rotating the logo at an angle for "dynamic" effect; adding a drop shadow, glow, or bevel; placing the Ink Charcoal wordmark on a busy photographic background without sufficient contrast; recoloring the logo to match a marketing campaign's seasonal palette; combining the icon mark and wordmark in a new, ad hoc lockup not specified above; cropping the logo tightly against the edge of an asset.

## 5. Imagery Guidelines

Imagery is one of the fastest ways a brand either reassures or triggers the "is this a dating app" fear, because photography carries an enormous amount of implicit signal about who a product is "for" and what kind of gathering is depicted.

**Principles:**

- **Real over staged.** Photography must depict real or realistically-styled small gatherings of people actually eating, talking, and sitting at a table together — genuine mid-conversation moments, imperfect lighting, real food, rather than glossy, evenly-lit stock photography of models staring at the camera holding wine glasses. Glossy stock photography is one of the fastest ways to make a hospitality brand feel fake.
- **Age-diverse, always — with a concrete minimum, not just an aspiration.** Every photo shoot brief must specify a required age-band spread before the shoot is booked, not leave it to whoever happens to be cast: **at least three distinct age decades represented across any image set of six or more people used in a single asset (e.g., App Store screenshot, hero banner, ad set), with no single decade making up more than 50% of the people depicted, and at least one person who reasonably reads as 45+ in every hero image used in paid or App Store contexts.** This is a required line item in the photo shoot brief template and a required check in the asset-review checklist before any image ships to `MARKETING.md`, `SEO.md`, or the App Store listing — not a subjective "does this feel diverse enough" judgment call left to whoever is approving the asset that week. No campaign image set should depict only people who look like they're in their twenties; this is a direct, non-negotiable response to the persona concern that youth-only imagery signals "not for you" to Devon and Grace, and youth-only imagery is also simply inaccurate to who the product serves. Any shoot brief or stock-photo selection that cannot satisfy this minimum must be rejected and re-cast, the same way an asset with the wrong logo color would be rejected.
- **Culturally and racially diverse, always.** Photo sets must reflect a genuinely global range of ethnicities, cultural dress, and table/food traditions — consistent with the "global-first, not US-first" value in `VALUES.md`. A Table in our imagery might show a shared hot-pot meal, a tapas spread, a potluck of casserole dishes, or a simple coffee-and-pastry gathering; it should never implicitly default to one cuisine or one cultural table-setting norm as "the" TableCrew table.
- **Groups, not couples.** No marketing image may depict a single pair of people in a way that could be read as a romantic date (candlelit two-top, wine glasses clinked between exactly two people gazing at each other). Every hero image defaults to three or more people at a table, which is both true to the product (Tables range 2–8 people, with the recommended size varying by activity per `PRODUCT.md`) and a direct visual firewall against dating-app misreading.
- **Real settings over aspirational sets.** Home kitchens, casual restaurants, community centers, backyard tables, and modest apartments are preferred over aspirational rooftop-bar or luxury-restaurant settings, which can feel exclusive or unattainable and undercut the "anyone, anywhere" language in our mission statement.
- **Candid body language.** Photography should show people mid-laugh, mid-pass-the-dish, leaning in to hear someone — not posed, camera-aware smiling. Candid body language is one of the strongest visual signals that a moment is genuine rather than performative.
- **Alcohol is never the visual subject.** Per `LEGAL.md` §4, food is always the visual and narrative center of any TableCrew photography; a glass of wine or a beer may appear incidentally on a table alongside food the way it would in any real dinner photo, but no marketing image may center a drink (a raised glass, a toast, a bar backdrop, a lineup of cocktails) as the focal point or headline visual of an asset. This is a direct extension of the "groups, not couples" and "real settings" principles above: imagery that reads as being *about* drinking rather than *about* the table carries the same liability and brand-tone risk as gamified alcohol copy (see `COPY_GUIDELINES.md` section 8) and must be rejected in asset review on the same basis.

## 6. Dos and Don'ts for Brand Usage

**Do:**
- Lead with the Table + Crew etymology (section 1) whenever introducing the brand to press, investors, or new users in long-form contexts (About page, `INVESTOR_OVERVIEW.md`, App Store "About the Developer" copy).
- Use age- and culturally-diverse, candid group photography in every marketing asset, per section 5, including paid social creative referenced in `MARKETING.md` and organic search-landing imagery referenced in `SEO.md`.
- Keep marketing headlines in the same plain, warm, unhurried voice as in-product copy (see `COPY_GUIDELINES.md`) — a landing page should not suddenly switch into aggressive growth-marketing language ("10x your social life!") just because it's outside the app.
- Explicitly and proactively state platonic, structured, no-agenda framing in any marketing copy or SEO metadata that could otherwise be ambiguous (e.g., a page targeting "meet new people in [city]" search terms should include language like "no dating, no swiping — just a seat at the table" near the top of the page).

**Don't:**
- Don't use neon, gradient, or pink/purple color treatments in any marketing creative, even for a limited-time campaign — this includes seasonal promotions, paid ads, and social graphics referenced in `MARKETING.md`; campaign creative must draw from the same palette defined in `DESIGN_SYSTEM.md`.
- Don't depict a lone couple in a romantic framing in any hero image, ad creative, or App Store screenshot.
- Don't use urgency, scarcity, or FOMO-driven marketing copy ("Don't miss out," countdown timers, "X people are looking at this Table right now") — this is a dark pattern prohibited by `VALUES.md` regardless of whether it appears in-product or in an ad.
- Don't let the brand voice drift toward hustle-culture or growth-hacking language in investor or press materials; `INVESTOR_OVERVIEW.md` should describe the business ambitiously and honestly without adopting a tone that contradicts the calm, hospitable brand the product itself embodies.
- Don't introduce a second display typeface, icon mark, or color palette for a special campaign "just this once" — brand consistency across every touchpoint listed in the repo (marketing site, SEO landing pages, App Store, investor deck) is what builds the trust this category badly needs.

## 7. Where These Guidelines Apply

This document governs brand expression everywhere TableCrew appears publicly. It is the direct reference for:

- **`MARKETING.md`** — campaign concepts, ad creative, email marketing, and partnership co-branding must be checked against sections 3 (voice), 5 (imagery), and 6 (dos/don'ts) before launch.
- **`SEO.md`** — landing page copy, meta descriptions, and content marketing must reflect the voice attributes in section 3 and must proactively address the "not a dating app" framing described in section 6 wherever search intent could be ambiguous (e.g., "make new friends as an adult," "meet people after moving to a new city").
- **`INVESTOR_OVERVIEW.md`** — the name story (section 1) and brand archetype (section 2) should inform how the company narrative is told to investors, since brand differentiation from the crowded dating/social category is itself part of the investment thesis.
- **`DESIGN_SYSTEM.md`** — the color, type, and imagery principles here are executed at the pixel level in that document; the two should never diverge.
