# TableCrew: Investor Overview

## 1. Company Snapshot

TableCrew is a platform that helps people build meaningful real-world relationships through small, intentional gatherings over coffee, lunch, and dinner — removing the friction of organizing them, with existing friends or curated new people, to fight loneliness and help people build a recurring social life. Its primary current surface is a mobile app (Flutter + Firebase), but the company is the platform — the trust and safety infrastructure, host and venue relationships, and brand — not a single app; see `docs/PRODUCT.md` for why this distinction matters for how we describe ourselves. Our mission is to make it effortless for anyone, anywhere, to build real friendships around a table. Our monetization is explicitly not ad-supported: we make money when people keep showing up to real tables, not when they keep opening an app.

This document summarizes the problem, the market, our product and business model, the competitive landscape, our roadmap and milestones, our use of funds for this raise, our team, and the risks we see honestly rather than glossed over.

## 2. The Problem

### 2.1 A public-health-scale loneliness problem

Loneliness has moved from an anecdotal concern to a formally recognized public health issue. The U.S. Surgeon General's 2023 advisory on the "epidemic of loneliness and isolation" documented that a large share of American adults report measurable loneliness, and drew an explicit comparison between the mortality impact of chronic loneliness and that of smoking or obesity — a striking reframing of a "soft" social problem as a hard health and economic one. This is not a US-specific phenomenon: comparable findings have been documented in the UK (which appointed a Minister for Loneliness in 2018), Japan (which appointed a Minister of Loneliness in 2021), and across other high-income, urbanizing societies where the same underlying drivers — smaller households, higher mobility, weaker default community institutions — are present.

### 2.2 Coordination friction is a distinct, addressable sub-problem

Loneliness is a large, structural problem no single app will solve. TableCrew is not positioned to "solve loneliness" — we are positioned against a narrower, concrete, addressable sub-problem inside it: even people who *have* friends and *want* to see them more often routinely fail to convert that intent into an actual recurring gathering, because organizing one (finding a time, a place, a headcount that works, chasing RSVPs) is real, unpaid, thankless coordination labor that typically falls on one person per friend group. Separately, people who want to meet new people as adults lack a low-stakes, structured, trust-safe format to do so — the informal, incidental ways this happened earlier in life (school, dorms, shared early-career social scenes) largely disappear by one's late twenties. TableCrew addresses both halves of this with one product: Crews for the first problem, Discover for the second.

### 2.3 Why now: three converging trends

**Trend 1 — Public-health prioritization is creating both consumer demand and future institutional willingness to pay.** The Surgeon General advisory and its international analogs are not just awareness campaigns; they are early signals of a policy and institutional shift. Employers, universities, and (in the medium term) health insurers and public health systems are increasingly treating social connection as a measurable wellness input worth budgeting for, not a soft perk. This is the structural precondition for our Phase 4 TableCrew for Teams offering and for longer-horizon institutional partnerships — the market is being primed to pay for social-connection infrastructure before we need that revenue line to exist.

**Trend 2 — Remote and hybrid work have permanently reduced incidental workplace socializing.** Regardless of where the remote-work-share number settles long-term, a large share of the adult workforce now has meaningfully less incidental daily social contact than the pre-2020 baseline — no shared commute chatter, no default lunch group, no hallway conversation. This is a durable, non-cyclical shift in the daily social-contact baseline for exactly our Devon persona (Remote-First Employee) and a contributing factor for Maya and Alex as well, and it is why "recurring social plans" now require deliberate structure rather than happening as a byproduct of where people already are.

**Trend 3 — A documented generational shift toward smaller, higher-trust group spaces, now applying to the physical world.** Younger users have visibly moved away from large public broadcast platforms toward smaller, closed, high-trust spaces online (private group chats, close-friends story features, Discord servers, invite-only communities) as their preferred mode of digital social interaction. TableCrew is the physical-world analog of that same shift: Crews are the "close friends group chat" of real-world gathering, and Discover is built around small, curated groups rather than large public events, meeting a preference shift that is already well underway rather than trying to create a new behavior from scratch.

## 3. Market Sizing

We size the opportunity from first principles, stating assumptions explicitly rather than presenting false precision. All figures below are directional, order-of-magnitude estimates intended to demonstrate the reasoning, not audited market-research figures.

**Total Addressable Market (TAM).** We start from the global urban adult population in the roughly 22-65 age range living in metro areas above ~500,000 people — the demographic and geographic zone where TableCrew's density-dependent model can plausibly operate (very small towns lack the venue density and population turnover for a two-sided local marketplace; the product also assumes smartphone-based, app-mediated coordination). We estimate this population at roughly 1.2 billion people globally, based on UN urbanization data showing well over half of the world's ~5.7 billion adults now live in urban areas, combined with the working-age/socially-active-age skew within that urban population. This is our TAM in the broadest, most theoretical sense: everyone who is structurally the right kind of person, in the right kind of place, to plausibly use a product like TableCrew someday.

**Serviceable Addressable Market (SAM).** We narrow TAM by the share of that population who report actively wanting more social connection or friendship in their lives — a figure repeatedly found in the 40-60% range across surveys referenced in the loneliness research summarized in Section 2.1 and detailed further in `USER_RESEARCH.md` (for example, large-sample surveys on adult friendship in the US have found roughly half of adults reporting fewer close friendships than they'd like, and comparable "want more social connection" findings recur across UK and other high-income-market surveys). Applying a conservative 40% to our 1.2 billion TAM yields a SAM of roughly 480 million people globally who are both structurally reachable and motivationally receptive to a product like TableCrew.

**Serviceable Obtainable Market (SOM).** This is where a multi-year, city-by-city rollout (per `ROADMAP.md`) meaningfully compresses the realistic near-to-medium-term number. We are not distributing to 480 million people in the next five years — we are building density in a bounded number of cities, one at a time, per the density-first logic in `MARKETING.md`. A realistic five-year SOM assumption: density-ready operations in 40-60 metro areas (consistent with Phase 2's 25-city milestone plus continued Phase 3 international expansion), each with a mature-market penetration target of roughly 1-2% of that city's addressable adult population actively using TableCrew in a given year (a deliberately conservative bar relative to category-defining consumer social products at maturity, reflecting that TableCrew's density-gated, community-ops-led rollout is intentionally slower than a viral, uncapped consumer app). Averaging a metro population of ~3-5 million addressable adults per launched city across a mix of large and mid-sized metros, 50 cities at 1.5% penetration yields an estimated **five-year obtainable user base in the 2-4 million range**, growing toward tens of millions over the full ten-year, 300+-metro vision described in `VISION.md` as density (not merely presence) is achieved across a larger footprint. We treat this SOM figure as the basis for near-term revenue planning; the SAM/TAM figures above establish that the ceiling on this business is not the constraint — execution speed and marketplace-liquidity discipline are.

## 4. Business Model

TableCrew generates revenue through three complementary streams (detailed in `PRODUCT.md`), deliberately excluding advertising as a matter of company values, not merely business strategy — an ad-supported model would create a direct incentive to maximize time-in-app and engagement, which is in tension with our actual product goal of getting people to spend time at a real table instead of in the app.

1. **TableCrew+ (consumer subscription)** — unlimited hosted Tables, priority Discover matching, no per-booking service fee, and advanced Crew scheduling tools. This is the primary revenue driver at maturity, reflecting a direct-to-consumer subscription model with predictable, recurring revenue tied to actual product usage (hosting and organizing), not to ad impressions.
2. **Venue partnerships** — referral and booking commissions from restaurants and venues receiving TableCrew-driven reservations, plus a "Featured Venue" placement product that lets venues pay for visibility on venue-selection surfaces (e.g., "suggested venues for your Table") during off-peak periods (e.g., weeknight dinners). Per `VALUES.md`'s explicit line-drawing on this exact product, this placement is always labeled "Sponsored," is capped so it cannot crowd out organic venue suggestions past a defined share of results, and is structurally walled off from Discover's person-to-person matching — a venue can pay to be suggested as a place to eat, never to influence who a user is matched or grouped with. That constraint makes this a narrower, lower-ceiling revenue product than an unconstrained behavioral-ad placement would be, which is the deliberate tradeoff for keeping it consistent with the company's stated values rather than a loophole.
3. **TableCrew for Teams (B2B2C)**, introduced Year 3+ per `ROADMAP.md` Phase 4 — a product for employers and universities to run structured small-group social-connection programs for their employees or students, monetized as a per-seat or per-program enterprise contract. This stream benefits directly from Trend 1 in Section 2.3 (institutional willingness to pay for measurable social-connection outcomes) and is sequenced last because it depends on TableCrew already having a proven consumer product and a credible trust/safety track record to sell into an institutional buyer.

Revenue mix is expected to shift over time: subscription revenue dominates early and at scale, venue partnership revenue grows steadily as city density increases (more Tables booked through more partner venues), and TableCrew for Teams becomes a meaningful, higher-margin, lower-volume-but-higher-ACV contributor from Phase 4 onward.

**A realistic view of venue partnership sales friction.** We do not model venue partnership revenue as a frictionless line item, and an investor should not either. Independent restaurants are a fragmented, low-tech, owner-operator-run industry with thin net margins (commonly single digits), and many venues already pay commission to one or more third-party reservation or delivery platforms — a new commission ask competes for a limited slice of already-thin margin, and the sales cycle runs through a single hard-to-reach decision-maker rather than a scalable, self-serve signup flow. Per `MARKETING.md` Section 4.2, venue partnership counts are a realistic output of sustained, in-person, community-manager-led relationship-building over weeks to months per venue, not a metric that scales the way a digital acquisition channel does. We therefore expect venue partnership revenue to lag city density growth, not track it linearly, and we model it accordingly rather than assuming commission revenue arrives automatically as soon as a city clears its Discover density threshold.

### Illustrative Unit Economics (TableCrew+)

The TAM/SAM/SOM walkthrough in Section 3 establishes a market ceiling; it says nothing about whether a subscriber is worth acquiring, which is the more immediate question for underwriting this raise. The figures below are explicitly illustrative and directional — stated with assumptions, not audited actuals, consistent with the standard set in Section 3 — and are meant to be replaced with real Phase 0/1 cohort data as it becomes available (per `SUCCESS_METRICS.md`), not treated as a finished model.

**ARPU.** `MARKETING.md` Section 5 anchors TableCrew+ pricing at $9-12/month. Assuming a blended average of roughly $10/month across monthly and any discounted annual plans, gross ARPU is approximately $120/year per active subscriber, before payment-processing fees (roughly 3% plus a small fixed per-transaction cost) and before promotional discounting from the referral program (`MARKETING.md` Section 5).

**Retention and LTV.** We do not yet have TableCrew-specific cohort retention data — this is a pre-launch/early-Phase-0 document. As a directional placeholder, we assume monthly subscription churn in the 4-6% range, broadly consistent with consumer subscription apps built around a genuine recurring utility hook rather than daily-engagement habit formation; TableCrew's positioning (tied to a recurring real-world ritual, not daily app opens) plausibly sits in the middle of that range rather than at either extreme, though this is an assumption, not a measurement. At 5% monthly churn, average subscriber lifetime is approximately 20 months, implying an illustrative gross LTV of roughly **$180-200** per subscriber (20 months x ~$10/month, net of payment processing).

**CAC, blended across channels.** Per Section 4 of `MARKETING.md`, paid acquisition is a secondary, later-stage channel; the majority of early users are expected to come through referral/word-of-mouth (Section 4.1) and community-manager-led local channels (Section 4.3), both structurally low-CAC but labor-intensive rather than free. Using `MARKETING.md` Section 5's own figures: a referral-driven subscriber costs roughly $9-12 (one free month, paid only on confirmed attendance), while a paid-social-driven install runs an estimated $15-40+ per install before accounting for install-to-attended-Table and attended-to-paying-subscriber conversion — a combined conversion rate that, for a product gated on real-world attendance rather than a simple signup, is plausibly well under 50%. That means the true paid CAC *per paying subscriber* (not per raw install) is more realistically in the **$60-150** range, not the headline per-install figure. Blending an assumed early-phase mix of roughly 70% low-CAC organic/referral and 30% paid (consistent with the channel-priority ordering in `MARKETING.md` Section 4), illustrative blended CAC per paying subscriber lands in the **$30-55** range.

**Payback period.** Against an illustrative ~$10/month net ARPU, a blended CAC of $30-55 implies a payback period of roughly **3-5.5 months**, and an LTV:CAC ratio of roughly 3.5:1 to 6:1 using the $180-200 LTV estimate above — a healthy ratio on paper. It depends, however, on two unvalidated assumptions: that the 4-6% monthly churn assumption holds once Discover-sourced Crews are included (see the marketplace disintermediation risk in Section 9, which could push real churn higher if users successfully take an established Crew off-platform), and that the paid-channel conversion funnel is not materially worse than estimated above. We present this as a reasoned, not a confirmed, unit-economics picture, and flag it as the first thing Phase 0/1 telemetry should validate or correct.

## 5. Competitive Landscape

We assess the landscape honestly, including where established players are genuinely strong.

- **Meetup.** The largest, most established player in "meet new people around an activity." Meetup's strength is broad category coverage and large public events at scale; its weakness relative to TableCrew is that it is built around larger, often one-off public groups and events rather than small, intimate, trust-curated gatherings, and it has essentially no mechanism for turning a good group event into a persistent, recurring friend group — there is no "Crew" concept. Meetup also carries a dated, utilitarian product experience that has not evolved to match how younger users now expect social products to feel.
- **Partiful / Posh.** Strong, well-loved products for event invitations among people who already know each other — beautiful invite experiences, RSVP management, and social sharing around a single event. Their limitation relative to TableCrew is scope: they solve the single-event invitation problem well but are not built around ongoing social-graph retention (no persistent Crew layer, no repeatable ritual/cadence mechanic) and are not designed for meeting curated new people at all — they assume an existing guest list.
- **Timeleft.** The closest direct analog to TableCrew's Discover half of the product — a dedicated stranger-dinner-matching product that has demonstrated real consumer demand for exactly the "curated dinner with new people" format. Its principal limitation relative to TableCrew is that it is a single-purpose matching product with no persistent Crew or friend-group layer: a Timeleft dinner is a one-off event, not the seed of a recurring social life, and it has no answer for TableCrew's other primary use case (helping existing friend groups actually gather more often). Timeleft validates market demand for our Discover mechanic while leaving the Crew/retention layer, and the combined-product thesis, entirely open.
- **Bumble BFF and dating apps generally.** Bumble BFF applies a proven pairwise-matching mechanic to platonic friendship, and dating apps broadly have normalized app-mediated meeting of strangers. Their structural limitation for our use case is twofold: they are built around one-to-one pairwise matching rather than small-group (2-8 person) gathering dynamics, and they carry romantic-context baggage (shared codebase, brand association, and user expectations inherited from dating products) that measurably hurts trust and comfort for a purely platonic, group-based use case — a meaningful share of potential users, particularly in older personas like Grace, are actively put off by anything that reads as "another dating app," even when the specific feature is platonic.

**TableCrew's differentiated position.** No competitor currently spans both halves of the problem: keeping existing friend groups actually gathering (Crews) and meeting curated new people in a small-group, trust-safe format (Discover), under one product with one persistent social graph. Our positioning is also structurally different in what we optimize for and measure: we anchor the entire product and business on real-world attendance (a Table Happened, a Table Rated) rather than in-app engagement metrics, which shapes both the product (headcount caps, lifecycle stages that map to real commitments, host reputation) and the business model (subscription and venue commissions tied to real usage, not ad impressions tied to time-in-app) in ways that are difficult for an engagement-optimized incumbent to simply copy without restructuring their own incentives.

**Defensibility against fast-follow competition.** The above is a static snapshot; a sharper question is how long it survives. The product mechanics described above — a Crew object, a Discover matching surface, headcount caps, lifecycle stages — are not, on their own, hard to clone. A well-funded incumbent (Bumble, Meetup) or a new entrant could plausibly ship a comparable Crew+Discover feature set within a few quarters of TableCrew demonstrating the combined model works, and we do not want to oversell the software feature set as the moat, because it isn't one. The real moat, consistent with the honest framing in `VISION.md`, is local density and liquidity plus accumulated trust infrastructure — neither of which a fast-follower can replicate on a feature-shipping timeline. A city that has already cleared the density threshold in `MARKETING.md` Section 3.2 (15-20+ weekly Open Tables, 60%+ fill rate, 25+ rated hosts) has real switching costs on both sides of the marketplace: hosts with an established reputation and rating history, attendees with an established Crew and Table history, venues with a working referral relationship — none of which a copycat can replicate by matching our UI. The honest risk we're naming, not solving, is this: a well-capitalized competitor that matches our density-first discipline city-by-city, rather than trying to out-spend us nationally on a thin product, would neutralize this moat. Our answer to that scenario is execution speed and a compounding trust/safety track record, not a structural barrier that guarantees the outcome — this is a real, live competitive risk, not a solved one, and it is the reason the density-first sequencing in `MARKETING.md` functions as a competitive-response strategy, not merely a growth-efficiency choice.

## 6. Traction Plan and Key Milestones

Milestones below are tied directly to the phase structure in `ROADMAP.md`.

- **Phase 0 (MVP, single city, Crew-only):** validate that Crews meaningfully reduce the coordination friction described in Section 2.2 for real friend groups in one dense, walkable city. Success is measured by Table-completion rate (Proposed → Happened) and repeat-Table rate within Crews, not raw signups.
- **Phase 1 (3-city launch, Discover introduced):** validate the harder, two-sided half of the model — that Discover can reach the local liquidity threshold described in `MARKETING.md` (15-20+ weekly proposed Open Tables, 60%+ fill rate, 25+ rated hosts per city) and that curated-stranger Tables convert to repeat usage and, ideally, into new Crews.
- **Phase 2 (Crew depth + recurring rituals, scale to 25 cities):** validate that the recurring-ritual mechanic drives durable retention (per `FEATURES.md`) and that the density-first city-launch playbook (`MARKETING.md` Section 9) is repeatable at a faster cadence across a larger number of markets without degrading per-city density quality.
- **Phase 3 (international expansion + venue partnerships):** validate the model outside its origin market and validate venue partnerships as a real, scaling revenue line rather than a pilot-only arrangement, consistent with the Global-first value informing internationalization timing.
- **Phase 4 (TableCrew for Teams, Year 3+):** validate the B2B2C thesis with a small number of anchor employer/university partnerships, converting the institutional-willingness-to-pay trend (Section 2.3, Trend 1) into an actual enterprise revenue line.

This raise is intended to fund the company through the Phase 0-to-Phase 1 transition and into early Phase 2 — the period where the core two-sided marketplace mechanic and the density-first playbook must be proven repeatable before a larger, later round funds more aggressive multi-city and international scaling.

## 7. Use of Funds (Seed / Early Round)

We allocate this raise across three categories, roughly in the following proportions, reflecting where risk and cost are concentrated at this stage:

- **Engineering (~45%).** Core product build-out (Flutter mobile app, Firebase backend) to complete the Phase 0 MVP and Phase 1 Discover matching system, plus the trust and safety infrastructure below. Because the technical stack is chosen specifically for velocity with a lean team (see Section 8 / `ARCHITECTURE.md`), this allocation buys more product surface per engineering dollar than a comparable custom-backend build would.
- **City launch and community operations (~35%).** Community-manager hiring and operating budget for Phase 0's single city and Phase 1's three cities, per the city-launch playbook in `MARKETING.md` — seed-host recruitment, venue partnership development, local content production, and the hands-on work required to hit each city's density threshold before Discover and any paid acquisition are switched on.
- **Trust and safety infrastructure (~20%).** Consistent with the "safety is a feature, not a department" value, this is funded as a core product line from day one rather than an afterthought bolted on after growth: identity verification tooling, host/attendee rating and reputation systems, incident-reporting and response workflows, and the underlying policy and moderation operations needed to responsibly operate the Discover marketplace (detailed further in `SECURITY.md`).

We do not allocate a dedicated paid-marketing budget in this round, consistent with `MARKETING.md`'s explicit sequencing of paid acquisition as a secondary, later-stage lever introduced only after a city clears its density threshold and unit economics are validated with real retention data.

## 8. Team

TableCrew is built by a lean, founder-led team structured around a founding CTO/PM pairing rather than a large early headcount, and this is a deliberate choice rather than a constraint we're apologizing for. The Flutter + Firebase technical stack (detailed in `ARCHITECTURE.md`) is specifically chosen to maximize engineering velocity for a small team: Flutter's single codebase for iOS and Android avoids maintaining two native codebases in parallel, and Firebase's managed backend services (auth, database, cloud functions, hosting) remove the need for early dedicated backend/infrastructure hires to handle work that is otherwise largely solved, configuration-level problems at this stage of scale. This lets the founding team's engineering effort concentrate on the genuinely hard, differentiated problems — Discover's matching logic, trust and safety systems, and the Crew/ritual retention mechanics — rather than on undifferentiated infrastructure. Community-manager hires (Section 7) are the first major functional hires outside engineering/product, reflecting that city-launch execution, not additional engineering headcount, is the binding constraint on near-term growth.

## 9. Risks and Mitigations

We state these plainly, because an honest risk assessment is more useful to an investor than a sanitized one.

**Chicken-and-egg marketplace liquidity in Discover.** Discover is a two-sided local marketplace, and like any such marketplace it faces a bootstrapping problem: without enough hosts proposing Open Tables, new users see an empty feed and churn before the marketplace can reach liquidity. *Mitigation:* we do not attempt to launch Discover broadly on Day 1 of a new city. Phase 0 is Crew-only specifically to avoid ever exposing a user to an empty Discover feed, and every subsequent city launch follows the explicit density-gating rule in `MARKETING.md` (Discover switches on only after concrete host/fill-rate thresholds are cleared), trading short-term growth speed for long-term marketplace health — a deliberate, values-consistent tradeoff rather than an oversight.

**Safety incidents.** TableCrew asks users to meet people — in Discover's case, sometimes strangers — in person, which carries inherent physical and personal-safety risk no product design can fully eliminate. A serious safety incident, especially early in the company's life, could cause disproportionate reputational and regulatory damage relative to a purely digital product's equivalent failure. *Mitigation:* safety is funded and staffed as core product infrastructure from the earliest stage (Section 7's 20% allocation), not layered on after a growth problem forces it; this includes identity verification, host reputation systems, incident reporting/response workflows, and deliberately conservative Discover rollout gating. We also accept, as a matter of stated company value, that this may slow growth in new markets relative to a competitor willing to move faster and looser — we consider this an acceptable and intentional tradeoff given the severity of the downside risk, not a nice-to-have we'll relax under growth pressure.

**City-launch execution risk.** The density-first, community-manager-led playbook that makes the model work is inherently more labor-intensive and slower to scale than a purely digital, self-serve growth motion — it depends on hiring and effectively managing good community managers city by city, a people-operations challenge distinct from (and in some ways harder to systematize than) a pure product/engineering scaling problem. A poor community-manager hire or a mismanaged city launch can waste months of runway in that market. *Mitigation:* the playbook in `MARKETING.md` is written as an explicit, repeatable operating sequence (seed hosts, prove Crews, layer in content and venue partnerships, gate Discover on density metrics, monitor for self-sustaining thresholds) precisely so that city-launch quality depends less on individual community-manager improvisation and more on a documented, iteratively-improved process; each city launch is also expected to sharpen the playbook for the next one, per the sequencing logic in `MARKETING.md` Section 3.3.

**Market education risk.** Both Crews and Discover ask users to adopt a new coordination habit (routing gathering-planning through an app rather than a group chat) and, for Discover, a new trust behavior (meeting curated strangers in a small-group dinner format). Neither behavior is completely novel — group chats already coordinate gatherings informally, and Timeleft and Bumble BFF have demonstrated appetite for structured stranger-meeting — but combining both into one habit-forming product is unproven at TableCrew's intended scale. *Mitigation:* persona sequencing (Maya and Alex first, per `USER_PERSONAS.md`) targets the users with the most acute, already-felt pain around exactly these two problems, rather than requiring TableCrew to create net-new demand from a cold audience.

**Dating-service / social-referral-service statute risk.** Several U.S. states — New York's General Business Law §394-c and comparable statutes in states including California, New Jersey, and Texas, per `LEGAL.md` Section 2 — regulate "dating services" and, in some formulations, broader "social referral services" that introduce members to each other for a fee, with requirements that can include background-check disclosures, specific contract-cancellation rights, and bans on default-renewal terms. Whether Discover is captured by any given state's statute turns on precise statutory language (fee-for-introduction, romantic-vs-platonic framing, membership structure) that has not yet been reviewed state-by-state, and Phase 1 explicitly launches Discover with paid TableCrew+ tiers active across three cities simultaneously — meaning this is a live compliance question at the exact moment revenue-generating Discover usage begins, not a distant hypothetical. We consider the absence of this risk from a typical stranger-matching investor narrative to be a red flag in itself, which is why we are naming it explicitly rather than assuming platonic framing resolves it. *Mitigation:* per `LEGAL.md` Section 2, this is now a named Phase 1 gating item in `TASKS.md` — counsel review of dating/social-referral-service statute applicability is required in each Phase 1 launch state before Discover goes live there, and any required disclosure or cancellation-right UI is built into Phase 1 product scope rather than retrofitted after a compliance gap is found.

**App store distribution and policy risk.** TableCrew is mobile-only with no meaningful web-based product alternative (Section 1), and it sits in a category — arranging in-person meetings between people who don't already know each other — that both Apple and Google apply meaningfully more review scrutiny to than a typical utility or content app, including age-rating questions specifically about user-to-user contact and meetup facilitation, and in some cases store-policy language governing apps that facilitate ongoing relationships or in-person meetups between strangers. Being held in extended review, required to make late-stage product changes to pass review, or in an adverse scenario suspended or removed by either store, is an existential distribution risk for a single-channel mobile product in a way it would not be for a multi-surface product. *Mitigation:* the identity verification, reporting, and incident-response infrastructure already funded as core product from day one (Section 7's trust-and-safety allocation, detailed in `SECURITY.md`) is substantially the same infrastructure both stores look for in this category, and store category classification and required safety disclosures are confirmed proactively with both platforms ahead of each new market's Discover launch rather than discovered reactively during a review rejection (see `SEO.md` Section 4.5 for the ASO-level treatment of this risk).

**Payment processor / merchant underwriting risk.** Stripe and comparable payment processors have historically applied heightened underwriting scrutiny to, and in some cases declined or terminated merchant accounts for, marketplaces that connect strangers for in-person meetings — a risk category with some overlap to how dating and companionship-adjacent services get flagged, even though TableCrew's platonic, group-based framing is materially different in substance. Losing payment processing capability, or having it delayed during underwriting, would directly block both TableCrew+ subscription revenue and venue commission payouts — a single point of failure for nearly all near-term revenue. *Mitigation:* rather than risk a post-launch account review or freeze, we plan to proactively and fully disclose the business model to our payment processor during initial underwriting — in-person group gatherings, identity-verified Discover matching, explicitly non-dating/non-romantic framing — and to maintain a secondary processor relationship in reserve through Phase 0/1 so that a single processor's risk-category reassessment cannot become a single point of failure for revenue collection.

**Seasonality and climate-driven demand variation.** Table formats tied to outdoor or seasonal activity (e.g., a "Sunday hike" Crew) plausibly see real demand drop-off in markets with harsh winters, which is not currently reflected in the traction milestones in Section 6 or the density thresholds those milestones depend on (`MARKETING.md` Section 3.2) — a city that clears its density bar in one season is not guaranteed to hold it year-round. *Mitigation:* per the fuller treatment added to `MARKETING.md` Section 3.4, this is addressed through interest-tag diversity across indoor and outdoor formats, a climate screen added to city-selection criteria, and rolling (not one-time) density monitoring — but we flag it here as a real, only partially-modeled risk to traction projections, not a solved one.

**Marketplace disintermediation risk.** Once a Discover match produces a good Table, the people involved have every incentive to exchange contact information and organize their next gathering directly (a group chat, a direct restaurant reservation) rather than routing it back through TableCrew — the same leakage risk every two-sided marketplace that introduces parties to each other eventually faces (compare Airbnb hosts and guests booking off-platform, or freelance marketplaces losing repeat engagements to direct relationships once the introduction has been made). This risk sits at the core of the product's own retention thesis: the Crew mechanic is explicitly designed to convert a one-off Discover match into a recurring, in-app relationship, but its success at doing so is unproven at scale, and the same product design that makes a Crew sticky and valuable (shared history, easy re-scheduling) is exactly what would make it easy to replicate for free outside the app once a group already knows each other. *Mitigation:* Crew scheduling tools, recurring-ritual prompts, and host reputation/rating history (per `PRODUCT.md`/`FEATURES.md`) are the product's structural answer — the goal is for the app to remain more convenient than a group chat, not merely necessary for the first introduction — but we do not yet have hard retention data distinguishing "Crews that stay in TableCrew" from "Crews that used TableCrew once to meet and then left," and we consider this an open, closely-watched metric for Phase 0/1 rather than an assumed non-issue. It is also the single biggest downside risk to the unit-economics churn assumption in Section 4.
