# Legal

**Added 2026-08 during Series A technical and legal diligence.** This document did not exist in the original knowledge base, and its absence was itself a finding: a consumer product that (a) verifies government ID, (b) introduces strangers to each other in person, (c) processes payments across borders, and (d) operates in multiple legal jurisdictions cannot responsibly ship without an explicit legal risk map. This is not a substitute for outside counsel — it is the internal document that tells engineering, product, and leadership what to ask counsel about, before a specific feature or market launch makes the question urgent. Every open item below is a real gating dependency for the roadmap phase noted, not background reading.

## 1. Entity, Terms of Service, and Privacy Policy

TableCrew needs a Terms of Service and Privacy Policy in force before any real user data is collected — `TASKS.md` already lists this as a pre-launch item, and this document does not relitigate that, only makes explicit what those documents must cover given the product's specific mechanics: platonic-intent framing and disclaimers (see Section 3), the two-tier verification model and what it does and doesn't guarantee about another user's identity or intent, the no-show and rating system's effect on account standing, the Featured Venue sponsored-placement disclosure (`VALUES.md`), and the data retention/deletion boundaries specified in `SECURITY.md` and `DATABASE.md`. **Recommendation:** engage outside counsel to draft ToS/Privacy Policy before Phase 0 launch (`ROADMAP.md`), and re-review both documents before Phase 3 (first international market), since a single U.S.-drafted ToS will not satisfy every jurisdiction's consumer-protection or e-commerce disclosure requirements. **Open item, gates Phase 0:** corporate entity structure (single U.S. Delaware C-corp initially is the standard, low-friction default for a venture-backed U.S. company, with foreign subsidiaries or a holding structure evaluated ahead of Phase 3's international entry) has not been finalized and should be decided with counsel and the cap table in mind, not by this document.

## 2. Platonic-intent framing is a legal question, not just a brand question

`docs/BRAND_GUIDELINES.md` and `docs/USER_PERSONAS.md` treat "this is not secretly a dating app" as a brand and trust concern. It is also a direct legal-classification question in several U.S. states: New York's General Business Law §394-c and similar statutes in states including California, New Jersey, and Texas regulate "dating services" and, in some formulations, broader "social referral services" that introduce members to each other for a fee — with requirements that can include background-check disclosures, specific contract-cancellation rights, and bans on default-renewal terms. Whether TableCrew's Discover feature is captured by these statutes depends on precise statutory language (fee-for-introduction, romantic-vs-platonic framing, membership structure) that varies by state and has not been legally reviewed here. **Why it matters:** if Discover is captured by a dating/social-referral-service statute in a launch state and we have not built the required disclosures or cancellation mechanics, that is a compliance violation from day one of Phase 1, not a theoretical future risk — Phase 1 explicitly launches Discover with paid TableCrew+ tiers active. **Recommendation:** have counsel assess dating/social-referral-service statute applicability in each Phase 1 launch state before Discover goes live in that state, and build any required disclosure or cancellation-right UI into the Phase 1 scope in `PRD.md` rather than retrofitting it after a compliance gap is found. This is now tracked as a Phase 1 gating item in `TASKS.md`.

## 3. Host and platform liability for what happens at a Table

A Table is a real, in-person gathering, typically at a venue serving food and often alcohol. Foreseeable harms include a fight or assault between attendees, a food-allergy or alcohol-related medical incident, a slip-and-fall or other venue-premises injury, or a car accident on the way home. **Why it matters:** without clear, counsel-reviewed liability allocation, a plaintiff's attorney will target whichever party has the deepest pockets and the most named involvement in the event — which, given that TableCrew's app actively matched the attendees, verified their identity, and (for Open Tables) suggested the venue, is a materially different liability posture than a passive listings site like Craigslist or a pure ticketing platform like Eventbrite. **Recommendation:** (a) ToS must include a clear, conspicuous assumption-of-risk and platform-is-not-a-party-to-the-gathering disclaimer, reviewed by counsel in each launch jurisdiction since enforceability of liability waivers varies significantly by state/country; (b) obtain a general liability / participant-injury insurance policy sized to cover a plausible worst-case in-person incident before Phase 0 launch, not deferred to Phase 1 scale — a single-city MVP still has real attendees at real venues; (c) an explicit "what to do if something goes wrong at a Table" in-app flow (emergency contact, local emergency number surfaced by the user's actual location, not just a US-centric "call 911") is a missing feature, now added to `docs/SECURITY.md` §9 and `docs/PRD.md`'s Discover requirements as part of this review pass. **Open item, gates Phase 0:** insurance underwriting for a "in-person stranger meetup" risk category is a nonstandard ask for most carriers and should be initiated early — this can take months to place, and should not be assumed to be quick to bind right before launch.

## 4. Alcohol

Many Tables will happen at restaurants and bars, and TableCrew's own copy examples (`docs/COPY_GUIDELINES.md`) reference dinner and drinks contexts. TableCrew is not a liquor licensee and does not serve alcohol directly, which limits (but does not eliminate) dram-shop-style exposure — that liability sits primarily with the licensed venue. **Why it matters:** the risk that does attach to us is different and narrower but real: if the app's own copy, marketing, or gamification (e.g., a hypothetical "drinks" interest tag or badge) is read as encouraging excessive drinking, or if a Table explicitly organized as a "bar crawl" or drinking-themed event leads to an alcohol-related harm, our proximity to the event as the organizing/matching platform is a plausible liability target even though we didn't serve the alcohol. **Recommendation:** `docs/COPY_GUIDELINES.md` and `docs/FEATURES.md`'s interest-tag taxonomy should avoid tags or marketing that center or gamify alcohol consumption specifically (versus "dinner," "drinks are optional and incidental," or similar neutral framing), and the ToS should include a standard "attendees are responsible for their own consumption and compliance with local law" clause. This is a low-cost, easy fix relative to the exposure it avoids, and has been flagged to the design/brand review group for `docs/COPY_GUIDELINES.md`.

## 5. Background checks and identity verification: what we can and can't consider

`docs/SECURITY.md` requires ID + selfie liveness verification for Discover, and a Serial Host / matchmaking-style framing invites the assumption that we also run criminal background checks (some competitors do, and some state dating-service statutes referenced in Section 2 may require disclosure of whether we do). **Why it matters:** if TableCrew does run or claims to run criminal background checks, U.S. federal (FCRA) and state "ban the box" / fair-chance laws impose specific procedural requirements (adverse-action notices, permissible-purpose disclosures, restrictions on how far back and which offense categories can be considered) that are materially different from — and stricter than — the identity-verification vendor relationship (Persona) described in `docs/SECURITY.md`. Conflating "we verified this person's identity" with "we ran a background check on this person" in marketing or in-app copy is both a legal exposure (implying a compliance regime we haven't built) and a user-trust risk (implying a safety guarantee we can't back). **Recommendation:** `docs/SECURITY.md` and all user-facing copy must be precise that Phase 0–2 verification is identity verification only (confirming the person is who they claim to be), not a criminal background check; if/when a background-check product is added (a reasonable future Trust & Safety enhancement, tracked as an open `TASKS.md` item), it must be scoped with FCRA/state-law compliance from the start, not layered on after the fact.

## 6. Content moderation and platform liability for user content

Table descriptions, chat messages, photos, and ratings are user-generated content. In the U.S., Section 230 of the Communications Decency Act provides significant (though not unlimited — it does not cover our own product design choices or claims) protection from liability for user-generated content; the EU's Digital Services Act imposes a materially different, more active due-diligence and transparency-reporting regime once we operate in the EU (Phase 3). **Why it matters:** `docs/SECURITY.md`'s moderation approach was designed against a general safety mandate, not against DSA's specific requirements (a public transparency report, a defined notice-and-action mechanism, a EU legal representative). **Recommendation:** treat DSA compliance as a named, resourced workstream ahead of any EU market entry in Phase 3, not an assumed extension of existing moderation tooling; flagged as an explicit Phase 3 gate in `docs/ROADMAP.md`'s legal readiness criteria (see the addition made to that document in this review pass).

## 7. Data protection: multiple regimes, not just GDPR

`docs/SECURITY.md` names GDPR, CCPA, LGPD (Brazil), and DPDP (India). **Why it matters, and what was missing:** each of these has different mechanics, not just different names — GDPR requires a named EU representative and (depending on processing volume/risk) may require a Data Protection Officer; CCPA gives users an opt-out-of-sale right distinct from GDPR's consent-first model; several jurisdictions (notably China, Russia, and a growing list of others) impose data-localization requirements that Firebase's regional configuration cannot always satisfy for a "certified in-country provider" requirement (`docs/ARCHITECTURE.md` §7 already names this as a migration trigger, correctly, for the technical side — this document names the legal-review side). **Recommendation:** maintain an explicit, counsel-reviewed jurisdiction checklist before each new-market entry rather than treating "we support GDPR/CCPA-style deletion" as a general-purpose compliance answer; explicitly exclude, until reviewed, launch in any jurisdiction with a data-localization requirement Firebase cannot satisfy (e.g., current-generation China market entry is out of scope for the foreseeable roadmap, and should be stated as such rather than left ambiguous).

## 8. Minors and age gating

The age-gating decision (13+ vs. 18+) is listed as open in `TASKS.md`. **Why it matters more than a typical open product decision:** given that TableCrew's Discover feature arranges in-person meetings with unverified-relationship strangers, any age threshold below 18 introduces COPPA (U.S., under-13) and a materially higher duty-of-care and liability profile that most peer companies in this category avoid entirely by setting a hard 18+ floor. **Recommendation:** set the platform-wide minimum age at 18, full stop, with no lower "Crew-only, no Discover" tier for minors — the operational and legal cost of maintaining a partial-access system for minors (age verification, parental consent flows, COPPA-compliant data handling) is not justified by the addition to the addressable market, and it removes an entire category of Trust & Safety risk (an adult and a minor being matched via Discover) that would otherwise require its own dedicated safeguard. This closes the open item in `TASKS.md`; if this recommendation is not accepted, the alternative requires a materially expanded Trust & Safety and legal scope not currently budgeted anywhere in this knowledge base.

## 9. Arbitration, class-action waiver, and dispute resolution

Not previously addressed anywhere in the knowledge base. **Why it matters:** for a consumer app with meaningful physical-safety exposure (Section 3), the dispute-resolution clause in the ToS is a first-order business decision, not boilerplate — mandatory arbitration with a class-action waiver is standard practice for consumer apps in this risk category and meaningfully changes litigation exposure, but enforceability varies by jurisdiction (several state and federal courts have narrowed arbitration-clause enforceability specifically for personal-injury and sexual-assault claims in recent years, an active and evolving area of law as of this writing). **Recommendation:** have counsel draft the dispute-resolution clause with current case law in mind rather than reusing a generic template, and revisit it if the legal landscape shifts materially before each major market launch.

## 10. Intellectual property

**Open item, low urgency but should not be forgotten:** "TableCrew" as a name and mark has not been confirmed as available for trademark registration in the U.S. or in planned Phase 3 international markets. **Recommendation:** run a trademark clearance search before any material marketing spend under the name (i.e., before Phase 0 public launch, not after), since a conflict discovered post-launch is far more expensive to resolve than one caught pre-launch.

## 11. India-first launch: this document was written assuming a U.S.-first home market, and that assumption is now wrong

**Added 2026-08.** `docs/ROADMAP.md` now names Hyderabad, India as the Phase 0 anchor city — India is the home market from day one, not a Phase 3 international expansion target. Sections 1–10 above were reasoned largely from a U.S.-regulatory starting point (Delaware C-corp default, FCRA/ban-the-box, NY-style dating-service statutes, Section 230, COPPA). None of that reasoning is wrong for a future U.S. entry, but it is no longer the first, gating jurisdiction, and re-review is a real Phase 0 dependency, not a Phase 3 one:

- **Entity structure:** an Indian private limited company (or a Delaware parent with an Indian subsidiary, a common structure for venture-backed companies planning to raise primarily from U.S./global investors while operating first in India) needs to be decided with counsel before Phase 0, not assumed to be the single Delaware C-corp Section 1 defaulted to.
- **Data protection:** India's Digital Personal Data Protection Act (DPDP), already named in `docs/SECURITY.md`, is now the primary, first-day data protection regime, not a secondary one reviewed ahead of a later international expansion — this changes the priority order of the jurisdiction checklist in Section 7 above.
- **Content/intermediary liability:** India's IT Rules (Intermediary Guidelines and Digital Media Ethics Code) impose their own due-diligence, grievance-officer, and takedown-timeline obligations on platforms hosting user-generated content, distinct from both Section 230 (Section 6 above) and the EU's DSA — this needs its own review before Phase 0 launch, not deferred to a hypothetical EU entry.
- **Age of majority and consent:** 18 is also the age of majority in India, so the Section 8 recommendation (18+ platform-wide) holds without modification.
- **Identity verification vendor coverage:** `docs/SECURITY.md` names Persona for ID + liveness verification; Persona's Aadhaar/India-ID coverage should be confirmed directly with the vendor before Phase 0, not assumed from its general market presence — if coverage is inadequate, an India-specific KYC vendor (several exist, built specifically around Aadhaar e-KYC) may be needed as a regional substitute, which is exactly the kind of per-region vendor exception `docs/ARCHITECTURE.md` §7 already anticipates in the abstract for data-residency triggers.
- **Payments:** Stripe's product availability and payment-method coverage (UPI is the dominant everyday payment rail in India, materially different from the U.S. card-first assumption baked into `docs/API_SPEC.md`'s Stripe-centric payment design) needs review before bill-splitting ships. **Two updates, 2026-08:** (1) bill-splitting was previously (incorrectly) scoped to Phase 0; it is now Phase 1 (`docs/ROADMAP.md`), which gives this review more runway but does not remove the requirement — it must still be resolved before Phase 1, not left open indefinitely. (2) The Firestore region question this bullet used to flag as open is now decided: `docs/ARCHITECTURE.md` §7 commits `tablecrew-prod` to the regional location `asia-south1` (Mumbai), since no India-inclusive multi-region Firestore location exists. The Stripe/UPI payment-rail question remains genuinely open and is the correct thing for the payments/API owner to resolve next.

None of Sections 1–10 above should be deleted or treated as wasted work — they remain the correct playbook for the eventual U.S. and other international entries — but the phase-tagging in this document and in `docs/ROADMAP.md`'s "Legal and Regulatory Readiness Gates" section should be read as: **India-specific review gates Phase 0**, and the U.S.-specific analysis above gates whichever future phase first targets the U.S. market.
